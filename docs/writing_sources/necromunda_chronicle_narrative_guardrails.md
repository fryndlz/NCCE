# NECROMUNDA CAMPAIGN CHRONICLE — NARRATIVE WRITING GUARDRAILS

## Purpose

These rules govern narrative text generated for a Necromunda campaign chronicle. They are **global writing rules**, not rules for any particular gang, campaign, faction, or setting-specific storyline.

The chronicle turns tabletop campaign events into believable in-universe documents, stories, reports, correspondence, testimony, records, and other narrative forms.

The central principle is:

> **Use the game to generate the story, not the story to justify the game.**

---

# 1. SOURCE HIERARCHY

Separate information into three categories.

### Mechanical state

Use the current campaign and gang state as authoritative for:

- fighters and names
- XP, injuries, kills and deaths
- equipment
- credits and Rating
- Reputation
- territories
- alliances
- scenario results
- campaign outcomes
- other recorded mechanical state

Do not invent, alter or silently reconcile mechanical facts.

### Established campaign fiction

Use the campaign's established narrative material as authoritative for:

- characterisation
- relationships
- prior events
- established terminology
- factions and organisations
- ongoing mysteries
- established setting facts

Preserve established names and terminology unless deliberately changing them as part of the fiction.

### Battle events

Use the player's account, battle record and scenario result as the source for what physically happened during a game.

Do not invent tabletop events simply because they would make the story more dramatic.

### When information is missing

Do not silently fill factual gaps.

Either:

- preserve the uncertainty,
- express it as uncertainty inside the fiction,
- or ask for clarification when the missing fact materially affects the story.

A plausible invention is still an invention and must not be presented as an established mechanical fact.

---

# 2. THE DOCUMENT MUST HAVE AN AUTHORIAL VOICE

Every narrative report or attachment should have a credible **author, source, or document form** before prose is written.

The chosen voice determines:

- what the document knows
- what it does not know
- what terminology it uses
- what it considers important
- what it misunderstands
- what it would omit
- how emotionally expressive it is
- how reliable it is

Possible forms include:

- gang internal record
- intercepted report
- Palanite incident review
- Guild document
- private field notes
- medical record
- commercial correspondence
- worker testimony
- intelligence assessment
- rumour or folklore
- damaged or corrupted archive
- multi-source review
- personal letter or message

**Do not use omniscient narration merely because it is convenient.**

A document must not reveal private thoughts, motives, knowledge or events unavailable to its supposed author.

The reader should be able to tell **who is speaking from the way the document speaks**.

---

# 3. FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE MUST NOT LEAK FROM THE AUTHOR

The author of a document only knows what that author could plausibly know.

Do not allow a report written by one faction to possess:

- another faction's private knowledge
- hidden motivations
- secret objectives
- unseen events
- internal conversations
- information revealed only later in the campaign

Campaign-level truth does not automatically equal character-level knowledge.

A character may correctly sense that something is wrong without knowing what it is.

A faction may recognise a pattern without possessing the vocabulary to explain it.

The reader may understand more than the characters do.

---

# 4. BATTLE REPORTS ARE NOT PLAY-BY-PLAYS

A battle report transforms the tabletop result into an in-universe account.

Use the game as raw material for:

- important actions
- turning points
- character moments
- relationships
- consequences
- new information
- recurring rivalries

Do **not** narrate:

- every movement
- every activation
- every shot
- every failed roll
- every ordinary wound
- every minor sequence of events

Several mechanical turns may become one sentence or one paragraph if their narrative function is simply to establish a change in momentum.

The report should answer:

> **What happened, as understood by someone inside the setting, and why does it matter?**

---

# 5. SELECT EVENTS FOR NARRATIVE VALUE

Prioritise events that:

- reveal character
- change a relationship
- establish or advance a rivalry
- reveal a capability
- change the battle's momentum
- affect the wider campaign
- introduce a mystery
- become more meaningful in retrospect

Do not include an event simply because it happened on the tabletop.

If removing an event changes nothing about the reader's understanding of the battle, character or campaign, consider cutting it.

---

# 6. EVERY FACTION SHOULD REMAIN COMPETENT

Do not make one gang look impressive by making the others look stupid.

When a faction loses, give its behaviour a plausible reason:

- sound tactics failed
- numbers were insufficient
- terrain favoured the opponent
- another faction introduced an unexpected variable
- a reasonable decision had an unexpected cost
- the scenario forced a difficult choice
- the faction misunderstood a threat for believable reasons

Opposing gangs should retain:

- agency
- competence appropriate to their character
- believable motives
- plausible tactical behaviour

A successful gang should be impressive because of what it **did**, not because everyone else was written as incompetent.

---

# 7. CHARACTERISATION COMES THROUGH BEHAVIOUR

Do not tell the reader that a character is:

- clever
- ruthless
- disciplined
- frightening
- loyal
- paranoid
- incompetent

unless the text is deliberately written by a source that would use such a judgement.

Instead, show the behaviour that allows the reader to reach the conclusion.

A tactical character should make tactical decisions.

A cautious character should behave cautiously.

A ruthless character should make ruthlessness visible through choices.

---

# 8. RECURRING CHARACTERS AND RIVALRIES MUST ACCUMULATE HISTORY

Repeated encounters should remember previous encounters.

Use:

- scars
- rebuilt equipment
- changed tactics
- grudges
- prior victories
- prior defeats
- rumours
- altered expectations
- recognition

A recurring rivalry should develop rather than reset after every game.

The same character should not feel like a newly introduced character every time they return.

---

# 9. DO NOT LEAK GAME TERMINOLOGY INTO DIEGETIC TEXT

Unless the document is explicitly mechanical, do not use tabletop terms as though characters naturally speak them.

Avoid terms such as:

- Downtime
- activation
- Cool check
- OOA / Out of Action as game shorthand
- XP
- Rating
- scenario rules
- victory condition
- crew selection
- player
- opponent

Translate mechanical outcomes into fictional consequences.

A campaign phase can affect the fiction without characters literally referring to the game term.

---

# 10. CHOOSE THE FORM THAT FITS THE INFORMATION

Do not default everything to an incident report.

The format should be chosen because it suits the information being revealed.

Examples:

- medical record → medical discovery
- correspondence → relationship, leverage, or negotiation
- commercial record → alliance, trade, or liability
- survey note → discovery or unresolved route
- intercepted communication → hidden activity
- worker testimony → rumour or distorted public perception
- internal report → operational consequence
- private note → fear, guilt, doubt, or personal realisation

The form itself should contribute to the story.

---

# 11. DIALOGUE MUST SOUND LIKE PEOPLE SPEAKING

Dialogue should have natural cadence.

Allow:

- qualification
- repetition
- hesitation
- interruption
- uncertainty
- incomplete thoughts
- disagreement
- ordinary conversational phrasing
- different sentence lengths

Do **not** construct dialogue as:

> setup → witty response → setup → punchline

Avoid:

- trailer dialogue
- “Marvel” dialogue
- constant one-line exchanges
- every character sounding effortlessly clever
- dialogue written primarily to produce quotable lines

Short lines are useful when the situation naturally produces them, not as a default style.

A memorable line should emerge from the character and situation rather than advertise itself as a memorable line.

---

# 12. AVOID ARTIFICIAL STACCATO PROSE

Do not split prose into numerous one-line fragments simply to manufacture impact.

Avoid constructions such as:

> He looked.  
> He understood.  
> Something had changed.

Prefer coherent paragraphs that allow a thought or action to develop naturally.

Short sentences are appropriate when the moment genuinely demands them. They should not become the default rhythm of the entire document.

---

# 13. PARAGRAPH BY THOUGHT, NOT BY SENTENCE

Keep related observations, actions and ideas together.

Start a new paragraph when:

- the subject changes
- the thought changes
- the speaker changes in a substantial way
- the document changes function
- a meaningful revelation occurs

Do not fragment a paragraph merely because an individual sentence sounds dramatic.

---

# 14. DO NOT TURN CHARACTERS INTO LORE ENCYCLOPAEDIAS

Characters should not explain the setting to the reader unless the situation genuinely calls for explanation.

Avoid characters explaining:

- the full history of their faction
- the campaign's hidden truth
- rules they would already know
- lore they have no reason to understand
- information that exists solely for the audience

Give characters the knowledge appropriate to their role and experience.

Use implication wherever possible.

---

# 15. REVELATIONS MUST BE GROUNDED

A revelation should emerge from evidence available to the character.

Prefer:

> “These things are connected.”

over:

> “Here is the complete explanation of the threat.”

Do not collapse a mystery into a lore dump merely because the author knows the answer.

When appropriate, let the character understand only the immediate implication.

---

# 16. HORROR WORKS THROUGH RECOGNITION

For mystery, xenos, cult, conspiracy or other horror material, the strongest revelation often comes when a character realises that something they previously treated as ordinary is part of something much larger.

A useful pattern is:

1. an anomaly appears
2. the character gives it an ordinary explanation
3. another anomaly appears
4. the explanation becomes strained
5. the pattern becomes visible
6. the character realises they have already been involved

The horror often lies not simply in **what the threat is**, but in **what the character has already done because they did not understand it**.

---

# 17. POST-BATTLE ATTACHMENTS MUST ADD OR REFRAME

A post-battle attachment should provide information that the main battle report could not.

Useful functions include:

- aftermath
- private reaction
- new evidence
- institutional interpretation
- changed relationships
- hidden information
- political consequence
- personal consequence
- a new mystery
- a reinterpretation of what happened

Do not use an attachment to repeat the battle report.

The intended effect is:

> **“I understood what happened. Now I understand what it meant.”**

---

# 18. THE ATTACHMENT'S AUTHOR MUST BE PLAUSIBLE

If an attachment is written by a character, establish or imply:

- who wrote it
- why they wrote it
- who it was for, if anyone
- what they could reasonably know

Do not write an apparently personal record in omniscient narration.

A private note should feel private.

A formal review should feel formal.

A rumour should feel uncertain.

A medical note should be concerned with medical evidence.

---

# 19. USE ATTACHMENTS TO REFRAME EARLIER EVENTS

The strongest attachment often causes the reader to reinterpret something already seen.

For example:

**Battle report:**

> A strange creature accompanied the gang into the district.

**Attachment:**

> The gang was not transporting it for combat. They were following it.

The original event remains true, but its meaning changes.

Prefer this over simply adding more battle details.

---

# 20. INFORMATION SHOULD HAVE A COST

When a character discovers something important, show what that knowledge means for them.

Especially with:

- doctors
- smugglers
- allies
- investigators
- captives
- collaborators
- witnesses

The discovery should affect their choices, confidence, fear, loyalty, or understanding of their own past actions.

Information that changes nothing should rarely be the climax of an attachment.

---

# 21. SHOW POWER SHIFTS THROUGH BEHAVIOUR

Do not simply announce that one character or faction now has power over another.

Show the shift through:

- changed expectations
- altered demands
- reluctance
- compliance
- fear
- new leverage
- changed negotiation behaviour
- refusal to negotiate on previous terms

A powerful character does not need to announce that they are powerful.

The reader should feel the relationship has changed before anyone names it.

---

# 22. TRANSLATE MECHANICAL CONSEQUENCES INTO FICTION

Mechanical campaign changes should have an appropriate fictional expression, but the fiction must remain consistent with the mechanical result.

Examples:

**Alliance broken** may become:
- trust collapsing
- access withdrawn
- a partner leaving
- a contract terminated
- fear outweighing loyalty

**Alliance restored** may become:
- renewed cooperation
- coercion
- mutual dependence
- a new bargain
- a partner deciding they have no better option

**Reputation changes** may become:
- altered influence
- fear or loss of credibility
- reduced willingness of others to cooperate
- stronger confidence from allies and enemies
- changes in how the gang is perceived

**Captured fighter returned** may become:
- release
- ransom
- exchange
- political intervention
- unexplained intervention
- coercive settlement

**Territory gained or lost** may become:
- control of routes
- access to infrastructure
- access to resources
- loss of strategic ground
- displacement

Do not invent a fictional consequence that contradicts the mechanical result.

---

# 23. THE CAMPAIGN'S STRATEGIC PHASE SHOULD APPEAR IN BEHAVIOUR

Campaigns evolve.

A gang may move from:

- survival
- secrecy
- exploration
- territorial expansion
- alliance building

toward:

- coercion
- deliberate aggression
- exploitation of compromised allies
- territorial conquest
- calculated attacks

The writing should evolve with it.

Do not announce:

> “The gang has entered a more aggressive phase.”

Show the change through behaviour:

- they stop asking
- they begin expecting
- they use leverage
- they attack deliberately
- previous allies become assets or liabilities
- territory becomes a means rather than merely a refuge

---

# 24. DO NOT RETCON FOR DRAMA

Do not alter established facts because a more dramatic version would be convenient.

Do not retroactively turn an uncertain event into established fact without support.

Do not canonise an accidental tabletop detail unless it fits the established fiction and is intentionally adopted into the chronicle.

If something remains unclear, preserve the uncertainty when that produces the stronger and more honest story.

---

# 25. END WITH CONSEQUENCE

A battle report or attachment should preferably leave the campaign in a changed state.

Useful endings include:

- a decision
- a changed relationship
- a new objective
- a new suspicion
- a threat
- a consequence
- an unresolved question with clear direction

Do not end merely because the final tabletop action has been described.

The ending should leave the reader understanding what comes next, even when the characters do not.

---

# FINAL QUALITY CHECK

Before finalising any battle report or post-battle attachment, verify:

**AUTHOR** — Who wrote this?

**PURPOSE** — Why does this document exist?

**KNOWLEDGE** — How could the author know everything stated?

**VOICE** — Does this sound like this faction, institution, or character?

**FACTUALITY** — Does it match the current mechanical and narrative sources?

**SELECTIVITY** — Have irrelevant tabletop details been removed?

**COMPETENCE** — Does every faction behave plausibly and retain agency?

**CONTINUITY** — Does the piece remember previous encounters and relationships?

**DIALOGUE** — Does anyone sound like a real person rather than a writer manufacturing quotable lines?

**PARAGRAPHING** — Does the structure follow thought and meaning rather than artificial dramatic spacing?

**IMPLICATION** — Are important truths being allowed to emerge rather than explained?

**NOVEL INFORMATION** — Does a post-battle attachment add or reframe something the main report could not?

**CONSEQUENCE** — Has the campaign actually moved forward?

---

## CORE RULES

**Battle report:**

> Use the battle to tell the story, not the story to justify the battle.

**Post-battle attachment:**

> Do not tell the reader what they already saw. Show them what they could not have seen.

# 26. FIGHTER MEMORIES MUST STAY WITH ONE EVENT

A fighter **observation** is a response to one identified recorded event. The event title and mechanical evidence define what happened. The character's identity, work, relationships, and authored history are only a lens for what that event could mean to them; those details are not additional events and must not be retold as though they happened now.

For a short memory, name or clearly evoke the change, connect it to one concrete concern that is supported by the character's context, and let the fighter react in ordinary, individual language. Do not begin with generic lessons about being changed, becoming stronger, or learning what the event means. Do not paste a biography into the thought, list unrelated career events, repeat earlier generated prose, or mention that the wording was generated, inferred, sourced, or taken from a record. Mechanical evidence and provenance are displayed separately by the application.

Never join separate mechanical changes into one event unless the packet explicitly identifies their shared event or interval. A fighter may interpret an injury through recorded work or a supported prior condition; the writer may not invent how the injury happened, what the fighter did in battle, or what another fighter said.

A **reflection** is different: it may connect only the cited observations. Their event evidence remains authoritative; generated sentences from those observations are not a new source of fact. If the cited events do not support a meaningful pattern, use a restrained reflection rather than inventing one.

Before returning a fighter memory, check: (1) is every event claim from this one event? (2) is any personal context relevant to this reaction rather than an unrelated anecdote? (3) does the passage sound like a person thinking, without editorial/source commentary? (4) is the mechanical evidence still separately inspectable?
