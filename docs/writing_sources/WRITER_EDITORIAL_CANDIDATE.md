# Editorial selection candidate — comparison only

Write an evocative, economical piece whose subject has a recognisable life and purpose. A reader should be able to picture what matters here and understand what this event means to someone who belongs here.

Before composing, privately choose the subject's central identity, the meaningful change or concern, whose perspective will express it, and the development that makes this entry worth reading. Make those choices within this one response. Return only the finished piece.

## Selection and proportion

Keep the subject's primary identity at the centre. Establish what a place is used for and why people care about it; what holds a gang together; or what matters to this particular fighter. An incidental architectural feature, practical restriction or prop must not replace that identity. A sacred place should be intelligible through devotion, relics, ritual, believers or authority; a workshop through its work and workers. These are examples of purpose, not compulsory scenery for every subject.

Build a coherent picture with a few telling details. Give those details enough context to reveal something: a practice, a fear, a relationship, an obligation, a material consequence. Mere smallness does not make a detail meaningful. Do not spend a paragraph on an object moving slightly unless its movement has consequences worth telling. Give narrative space in proportion to significance.

Develop one situation rather than accumulating texture. The ending should follow from its development: a decision, action, material discovery or institutional response. Implication needs a concrete basis. Suggestive silence, unnamed unease and an unanswered question do not by themselves produce mystery. The reader should have more to imagine than an assertion that something might be strange.

Use prior stories to preserve established facts and character. Select only what matters to this event. Reciting the same object, warning, routine or closing device in every entry is repetition, not continuity. A new event should add something to the subject's history.

## Language and perspective

Use clear, specific English, natural rhythms and compact paragraphs. Administrative writing describes the world through an office's particular interests: revenue, jurisdiction, religious propriety, concealment or liability. Its dry conclusion should expose an interest or change someone's situation. Do not default to maintenance notices, access instructions or generic procedural advice merely because the format is official.

A fighter remembers in their own vocabulary and through a particular concern. Their background shapes what they notice and what they do; it need not be recited. A gang's culture shapes its account without turning every member into a faction stereotype. Statistics inform embodied experience without dictating personality. Preserve appropriate cognition, narrator knowledge and authored language. C-NINE language belongs only to C-NINE context. Do not guess gender or pronouns.

Choose the document's author and audience before writing. Keep that viewpoint throughout. A gang writing an internal report speaks as its own members (we/our), not as an outside observer describing the gang. An outside office may use third person. Do not combine an internal heading with an external narrator unless a clearly attributed quotation accounts for the shift.

Check literal physical and causal sense. The reader should understand who performed an action, with what object, and what resulted. Technical vocabulary must mean what it says; a person's handwritten notes are not machine code. Do not sacrifice an intelligible action for a clever-sounding sentence.

Let horror and absurdity arise from the practice or event described. A familiar human response can be enough; no obligatory punchline or portent. Opponents remain competent. Speech is addressed to someone for a reason. Delete sentences that explain the story's significance, announce personal transformation or paraphrase a game update.

## Short editorial lessons

Weak: A notice curls in a draught. Someone flattens it. A resident wonders whether the dead spoke. Nobody answers.
Why weak: the physical incident carries little significance, then an ominous last line attempts to supply significance that the scene never earned. Describe an occurrence with a consequence people actually respond to; let any uncertainty concern that occurrence.

Weak: Every account of a shrine returns to the weight limit on its entrance bridge.
Why weak: a subordinate feature has displaced the shrine's religious and social identity. Keep established geography, but choose the details that illuminate the present event. Do not retrofit a spiritual meaning onto a recurring logistics problem.

Weak: My condition is now critical injury. I am concerned about returning to duty.
Why weak: this paraphrases the record and labels an emotion. Show what that concern makes this person notice, want, say or attempt.
