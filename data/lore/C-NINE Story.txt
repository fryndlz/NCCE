THE C-NINE

[GEOTHERMAL PROSPECT DETAIL C-9]

THE PROSPECT
Daven Holt had been Foreman of Geothermal Prospect Detail C-9 for eleven minutes when he saw the three-armed man.
Holt's appointment had been routine. The previous foreman had retired, and the Labour Administratum wanted somebody to bring an increasingly irregular Detail back into compliance.
He had supervised mutants before. Anyone who worked the lower reaches of Hive Primus had. Generations spent among industrial contaminants, geothermal effluvia and whatever else accumulated beneath the habitable levels produced abnormalities that the Administratum generally found cheaper to classify than investigate.  
Even so, Jarek Voss required a moment.
Voss introduced himself as senior survey hand and offered Holt a hand. Holt took it. Neither man acknowledged the other two.
The personnel ledger described Voss as a reliable worker of twenty-eight years' service, with commendations for productivity, equipment recovery and an irritating number of disputes concerning evacuation procedure. He had repeatedly delayed withdrawal to recover injured labourers the Guild considered expendable. Holt soon discovered that this attitude extended throughout C-9.
The Detail was old enough to have become hereditary through administrative inertia. Children inherited labour obligations from parents, families occupied the same quarters for generations, and congenital abnormalities accumulated faster than medical officers could catalogue them. Some were difficult to overlook. K-117 and R-204 worked under restricted labour designations and protective clothing tailored so extensively that Holt suspected its purpose was discretion rather than safety. Heavy Labour Asset B-01, called Breaker by everyone except the paperwork, could pull fractured ferrocrete apart with his hands.
Voss had an explanation ready for every irregularity: hereditary mutation, industrial exposure, incomplete records, the usual catalogue of excuses produced by the lower hive. Holt could have accepted most of them individually. What bothered him was that he seemed to be the only person in C-9 who ever needed an explanation.  
C-9 seemed to operate according to customs nobody had written down. Tools appeared where they would shortly be required. Workers changed headings without instruction and later produced sensible reasons for doing so. A crew would halt because Orik Sorn disliked the stone, or remain at the cage because somebody from another shift had not returned. Voss seldom issued the orders Holt expected from him. Often he merely waited until the Detail arrived, unanimously, at the same conclusion.
Once, Holt found two children sitting beside Breaker during shift change. One was asleep against the creature's leg while Breaker remained perfectly still. Holt demanded to know why children had been allowed near a restricted heavy labour asset.
Voss looked genuinely surprised. "He's Crew."
That word appeared often in C-9. By the end of his first year Holt had found enough discrepancies to occupy an audit office for months: ventilation extending beyond authorised workings, unregistered dependants, medical exemptions issued under obsolete directives, survey maps continuing far past official limits. The maps concerned him most.
C-9 had spent years descending through the accumulated strata of Hive Primus. Their workings crossed abandoned habs, dead manufactoria, forgotten transit systems and foundations buried beneath later foundations. The deeper routes had little obvious geothermal value, yet the Detail continued extending them whenever time and equipment allowed.
They called the descent the Prospect. Holt could find no survey objective corresponding to that name.
"What are you expecting to find down there?"
"Don't know," Voss said. "That's why it's called prospecting."
Holt couldn't argue with that, but the answer irritated him enough that he recorded the exchange for his next compliance report anyway. 
+=+=+=+=+=[ 💀 ]=+=+=+=+=+
Months later, R-204 returned from another unauthorised deviation with news of a descending passage beyond a collapsed transit conduit. Standard procedure required it to be sealed pending assessment. Instead, Holt found C-9 preparing equipment. The Prospect, Voss explained, continued below. Holt went with them.
They descended beneath anything shown on his official charts, through chambers built into the remains of other chambers and foundations driven through architecture whose purpose had been forgotten. The deeper they went, the less stable those remains became. Floors had sagged into the levels beneath them. Support columns carried loads they had never been designed to bear. In places, entire sections of the old hive seemed to persist through little more than compression, corrosion and the accumulated weight of everything built above.
The heat rose with every level. Their instruments became unreliable, yet C-9 moved with the same infuriating certainty Holt had observed for months. At junctions they sometimes hesitated, but the decision seemed to settle through the group without discussion.
K-117 selected a passage before Garrick had finished surveying the alternatives. Holt caught Voss by the arm.
"How does he know?"
Voss looked at Holt. "He just knows."
"How?"
For a moment Holt had the strange impression that Voss pitied him. "You wouldn't understand."
Further down, Holt found something he did understand.
An ancient control housing protruded from one wall, half buried beneath mineral deposits. Holt recognised the markings from abandoned development plans he had spent months reviewing. Whatever machinery it had once governed was still under pressure. The indicator was faint, but unmistakable.
Holt reached for the emergency release.
Someone shouted his name.  
He pulled it.
Somewhere beyond the wall, something enormous moved.
The sound travelled away from them through the buried structure until it became impossible to distinguish from the groaning of the hive around them.
The last transmission from the expedition reported structural failure several hours later. What followed was never entered into the geological record. Holt remembered falling. He remembered heat and the taste of blood. He remembered calling for C-9 and hearing answers from passages where no one could possibly have heard him.
Then he remembered something holding his head very still, something he knew, with sudden animal certainty, was not human, and its breath against his face.
Afterward, there was no darkness. There was everyone.
Drenn somewhere behind him, frightened but alive. Garrick searching for another route. Breaker's slow, enormous presence further away. K-117. R-204. Even the children far above, sleeping in C-9 quarters. Hundreds of impressions arrived without words, separate and inseparable, until Holt could no longer understand how silence had ever seemed empty.
Behind them all was another presence, immense in a way that had nothing to do with physical size, something around which every one of those lives bent, and which now bent Holt toward itself.
Holt understood. He understood why nobody left while another worker remained below. Why Breaker was safe with the children. Why R-204 knew which passage to take. Why conversations had stopped when Holt entered the room, although nobody had been hiding anything from him.  
For more than a year he had believed C-9 were keeping a secret, but there had never been a secret. There had only been something he could not hear. And now, now he finally could, and knew himself heard in return. A single word rang through his head like a shift siren: Brood. Crew. One and the same.
When Holt opened his eyes, Jarek Voss was beside him. For the first time since Holt had arrived at C-9, Voss looked at him without that faint, patient distance.
"Foreman."
This time the title meant something different. Holt had spent a year buried in official surveys, abandoned development plans and revisions that C-9 had never seen. Now those records mingled with Garrick's maps, R-204's remembered passages and the accumulated knowledge of the Crew. The collapse had exposed something Holt recognised, and together they understood what none of them could have understood alone. There was a way forward.
Then something came back through the Crew, from Voss and Garrick, Drenn and R-204, from all of them at once. Holt did not have to read their faces or wonder what they meant. There was no distance left in which doubt could live, no possibility of misunderstanding what passed between them. He knew them, and was known in return, with a certainty more intimate than words could ever have given him.
They trusted him. Not the route. Him. C-9 had spent years searching for a way forward. Now they expected Holt to show them.
+=+=+=+=+=[ 💀 ]=+=+=+=+=+
Seven days after the expedition disappeared, Geothermal Prospect Detail C-9 returned to the registered workings. Jarek Voss led the survivors to the surface and accounted for every member of the returning party.
Daven Holt was not among them.
Asked whether Holt had survived the collapse, Voss answered that he had. Asked why the Detail had returned without its Foreman, Voss replied:
"He's where he needs to be."
No recovery party was requested. Voss assumed the duties of Foreman, and C-9 resumed the Prospect.
The route Holt had found carried the Prospect deeper than any previous survey. Garrick incorporated portions of it into the Detail's private charts while Griz extended ventilation after the advancing crews. The Prospect passed beneath the last recognised districts, then beneath structures so old that their foundations had become indistinguishable from the debris around them. Still the Prospect continued.
The name spread through C-9's records. Equipment was requisitioned for the Prospect. Shifts volunteered for the Prospect. Children were warned away from unfinished sections of the Prospect. Nobody recorded what would happen when it ended, although increasingly the Detail behaved as though an end existed and could be reached.
Eventually, it was R-204 who found it. He had been clearing the remains of an ancient foundation when his tools exposed a dark surface unlike anything around it. Mara Kel found no joins. Garrick found no corresponding structure on any chart. Griz checked for conduits and reinforcement and found neither. Voss scraped centuries of accumulated filth from the surface with the edge of a tool.
Stone. Natural stone.
For generations, C-9 had descended through everything Hive Primus had built, occupied, abandoned, buried and forgotten. They had followed the Prospect through the dead strata of Necromunda until there was nothing human left beneath them. Finally, they had found what they were looking for: nothing.   
Voss rested one hand against the rock while the Crew gathered behind him. The Prospect was complete. Now, at last, the real work could begin.

THE DEEP ANSWERS
The Sludge Sea concealed more depth than it had any right to possess.
For nine shifts, C-9 lowered probes through layers of chemical scum, drowned machinery and sediment accumulated over centuries of industrial runoff. The first two cables corroded through. The third returned coated in something Mara Kel advised everyone not to touch. By the sixth attempt they had learned to harvest the vapours gathering above the surface and compress them into canisters capable of choking a man to death. This was useful, but it was not why they were there.  
Before the fighting with the Slag Eaters, everyone in C-9 had felt something from the direction of the Sea. Faint enough that none could say where it began, but familiar in a way that direction and distance could not explain. Something had pressed against the edge of the Crew and, for a moment, seemed to answer.
The old Prospect had already reached natural stone beneath Hive Primus, yet the signals appeared elsewhere in the buried hive, separated by kilometres of collapsed districts and sealed foundations. So C-9 had begun prospecting sideways. The Toll Crossing opened one route, the Promethium Cache supplied another expedition, and the Old Ruins gave access to forgotten structures. Each acquisition made sense afterward in the language expected of a mining Detail. Before each came the same thing: a feeling, a direction, something almost heard. 
Before each came the same thing. A feeling. A direction. Something almost heard.The Sludge Sea had been the clearest yet. Then C-9 fought for it, took possession, opened every access they could find beneath the surrounding workings and found nothing. The signal never returned.
+=+=+=+=+=[ 💀 ]=+=+=+=+=+
Holt returned during the tenth shift. Drenn was supervising the lowering of another probe when his hands stopped moving. Mara looked up from the filtration assembly beside him, while elsewhere workers paused in corridors, workshops and quarters without knowing what had interrupted them. Someone had entered the Crew.
Recognition came slowly because the person they remembered was no longer quite the person returning. Holt had spent months beyond the registered workings, far from the constant nearness of hundreds of other minds, while C-9 had changed in his absence. The Detail he remembered had been cautious, secretive and accustomed to surviving by remaining beneath notice. The Crew he encountered now carried memories of gunfire and captured ground, injuries and new faces, territories won and enemies who had learned their name. They had grown harder and closer through use, and they carried losses too: Griz was gone, others bore wounds they had not possessed when Holt left, and there were memories among them that he had never lived. Holt understood all of this before anyone saw him.
By the time Daven Holt emerged from an abandoned service passage adjoining the Sludge Sea workings, introductions would have been absurd. He still wore the hazard suit in which he had disappeared, repairs covering repairs until little of the original seemed untouched, and he carried nothing else. But he had not returned alone.
At first the workers nearest the passage understood only that two presences were approaching through the dark. Holt was familiar despite everything that had changed in him; the other was not. It moved into the work lamps on four limbs and rose before them.
Mutation was ordinary in C-9. Additional arms, altered skulls and bodies distorted by generations in the deep were explained away by Guild physicians as hereditary contamination and industrial exposure, explanations whose usefulness Breaker had long ago exhausted. Yet nothing about the creature before them looked distorted or accidental. The arrangement of its limbs, the sweep of its carapace and the economy with which it moved seemed complete in a way the Crew were not. Features they had spent their lives concealing beneath hazard suits and work coats suddenly looked like incomplete approximations of something standing finished before them. Nobody reached for a weapon. The creature regarded them, and recognition moved through the Crew without belonging to any one person. They had been looking for this.
Questions followed about where Holt had been, what he had seen and whether there were others. Garrick asked perhaps three aloud; the rest passed through the Crew with Holt's answer. They remembered chambers beneath the natural stone, passages polished by movement and hollows lined with ancient organic matter. Shapes slept within them, some collapsed with age, others preserved well enough that Holt had learned not to approach. Months of his life arrived without sequence: darkness without loneliness, buried warmth, things sleeping and things that had died without waking.   
Beneath those memories was a purpose Holt had never been able to put into words because he had never received it in words. He had known where to go and, when the time came, what he was meant to bring back. As that understanding settled through the Crew, the creature beside him turned its head toward them and for an instant something impossibly distant seemed to turn with it.
The sensation vanished almost immediately. Afterwards, none of them could remember noticing it.
+=+=+=+=+=[ 💀 ]=+=+=+=+=+
Breaker came home three days later.
Nobody in C-9 seemed entirely sure who had paid for his release. The Van Saar received their money, somebody arranged transport, and shortly before the end of second shift a freight crawler stopped at the Toll Crossing with Breaker hidden beneath a stained tarpaulin in the back. The driver would not help unload him. The children did.
They were climbing over Breaker before both his feet had touched the ground. He gathered two of them into his arms while another immediately attached herself to his back, and for a while there was little room in the Crew for anything except the relief of having him close again.
Then Breaker noticed the creature.
It had come up from the lower workings and was watching from beyond the gate. Breaker slowly put the children down.
All his life, other people had looked at him and seen something that had gone wrong. His size, his arms, the tendrils around his mouth: Guild physicians had names for all of it, each describing another way in which his body had failed to remain properly human. C-9 had never cared. He was Breaker. He was Crew. But even their acceptance could not show him what he was supposed to be.
Now it stood before him.  
The creature approached, and Breaker recognised himself in it. The same shapes were there, but complete: four arms moving with effortless purpose, claws instead of malformed hands, a body in which everything that made Breaker an aberration seemed deliberate. He had spent his life measuring the distance between himself and humanity and finding himself wanting. He understood now that he had been measuring in the wrong direction.
Breaker sank to his knees. His shoulders shook, and tears ran freely between the tendrils of his face as the creature came close and rested one clawed hand against his head.
One of the children took Breaker's hand and looked up at it.
"Is it Crew?"
Breaker held the creature's claw against his head for a moment longer.
"Yes," he managed.
For the first time, Breaker knew himself not as something diminished from a human being, but as an unfinished image of something greater.
+=+=+=+=+=[ 💀 ]=+=+=+=+=+
Eventually Holt led them below. Beyond the last buried architecture, the old Prospect markers gradually disappeared into natural stone. At a branching passage Garrick stopped to examine the alternatives, but Holt had already chosen one and was disappearing into the dark. Jarek watched him go and remembered the Holt who had first come down with them, catching him by the arm in another buried passage and demanding to know how K-117 could possibly have known which way to go. This time nobody asked. They followed Holt.
They remembered the approach clearly, and Holt becoming quieter as they descended, although he had spoken little to begin with. His presence within the Crew seemed to stretch ahead of them, drawn toward something none of the others could perceive. Then there was a chamber, and beyond that their memories refused to settle into anything reliable.
Garrick remembered seeing a shape and, for one terrible instant, understanding that it was alive. Something in him recoiled before thought could intervene, seized by the animal certainty that whatever occupied the chamber was alien, impossible and profoundly wrong. Mara remembered taking a step backward before an overwhelming warmth dissolved the impulse to run. Drenn remembered lowering his eyes. Jarek remembered looking directly at something his mind refused to keep. The terror disappeared almost as soon as it arose, submerged beneath a sense of belonging so complete that the thing before them ceased to feel alien because, for those few moments, the idea that it could be separate from them became impossible to hold.  
Holt remembered more, not because he was stronger, but because something allowed him to. The presence that had always orbited the Crew's awareness settled around him with terrible intimacy, opening and moving through his thoughts with the ease of hands sorting tools on a bench. There was neither cruelty nor kindness in what touched him; those were distinctions meaningful only between beings separate enough to choose one over the other.
Whatever passed through Holt reached the others as something closer to memory than instruction. For a moment the buried world around them rearranged itself. Distance ceased to mean metres and rock ceased to mean obstruction. Scattered through the darkness beneath Necromunda were points of familiarity they had always lacked the faculty to recognise. Some were weak enough to be echoes, others impossibly distant, but a few were unmistakably alive: dormant presences folded inward upon themselves, waiting somewhere in the deep. C-9 recognised them with the same instinctive certainty with which they recognised one another.
Then it was gone. Jarek found himself beside Holt, unable to remember clearly who else had occupied the chamber or what they had come there to see. The gaps did not trouble him. Something vast and comforting remained just beyond conscious thought, leaving only the certainty that they had been seen, known, and changed.
By the time they returned to C-9, Garrick understood why the Sludge Sea survey had failed. They had searched beneath the signal because they had imagined it travelling through the hive like sound. Now the mistake seemed obvious. Stone, ruins and distance mattered to bodies; whatever they had been following obeyed another geography entirely. The presence they had sensed at the Sea might have been kilometres away, perhaps deeper, perhaps not beneath it at all.
They still had no map to what was sleeping under Necromunda. But when Garrick thought of the scattered impressions that had drawn C-9 from territory to territory, they no longer felt scattered.
For the first time, they knew which way to listen.
+=+=+=+=+=[ 💀 ]=+=+=+=+=+
That night Garrick opened the private charts and marked every place where the Crew had heard something beyond itself. Holt stood beside him as the scattered points resolved into a pattern neither could have recognised before. For the first time they could sense where the Prospect wanted to go next.
The rest of the chart looked different too. Routes opened during the search now belonged to C-9. Fuel, weapons and recovered machinery had accumulated around it. Krail had seen enough to understand the value of silence. Veyne had carried their sealed cargo farther than C-9 could ever have reached alone. Even the gangs surrounding them were no longer unknown quantities; the Crew had fought them, bled with them, learned how they moved and what they were prepared to defend.
Until now, C-9 had followed whatever traces it could reach and taken whatever the hive left unguarded. They had been searching almost blind, without understanding that every faint presence in the dark was another part of something scattered long before any of them were born. Now the Prospect had a purpose: find them, wake them, bring the family together.
They were not blind anymore.
Garrick looked at the next signal. There was already somebody else's territory between C-9 and it.
He drew the line anyway.
